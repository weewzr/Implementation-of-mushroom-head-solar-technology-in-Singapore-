\begin{abstract}
This project investigates three-dimensional photovoltaic geometries originating from a rotating mushroom-head concept for land-constrained Singapore. The principal hypothesis is spatial packing: additional active PV area may be placed above a constrained horizontal footprint while attempting to retain irradiation quality, sky visibility, ventilation and usable space beneath. No candidate geometry is assumed optimal. Exploratory calculations are distinguished from validated yield predictions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Solar photovoltaic power in Singapore: a beginner's picture}
A photovoltaic (PV) module is a surface made from semiconductor solar cells. Sunlight carries energy in photons. When photons are absorbed in a solar cell, they can create mobile electrical charge carriers; the cell's internal electric field separates those charges and produces direct-current (DC) electrical power. An inverter then converts the DC output into alternating current (AC) suitable for a building or the electricity grid. PV therefore converts part of the incoming solar radiation directly into electricity; it is not a heat engine and it does not require the panel to become hot.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includesvg[width=\textwidth]{../figures/how_solar_pv_works_singapore}
\caption{Beginner-level picture of the solar-energy chain used by this project. Sunlight reaches a PV surface as a combination of direct and sky-diffuse radiation; the module produces DC electricity and an inverter converts it to AC. The cloud geometry is conceptual, not a measured Singapore weather record.}
\label{fig:solar-pv-beginner}
\end{figure}

For geometry, the important point is that sunlight is not one single number. Global horizontal irradiance (GHI) is the total short-wave solar power received by a horizontal surface. It can be separated conceptually into a direct-beam part and a diffuse part scattered by the atmosphere and clouds. Direct normal irradiance (DNI) describes the beam arriving from the direction of the Sun, while diffuse horizontal irradiance (DHI) describes radiation arriving from the rest of the sky. This distinction matters because tilting, curving or rotating a PV surface changes its angle to the direct beam and its view of the diffuse sky.

Singapore is close to the equator, so the Sun can reach high elevations and usable daylight is available throughout the year. At the same time, Singapore's tropical atmosphere is frequently cloudy and rainy, so instantaneous solar input can change rapidly and diffuse sky radiation is important. The project therefore cannot judge a geometry only from a clear-sky noon picture. A fair annual comparison eventually needs time-resolved Singapore irradiance and weather, including the direct/diffuse split, together with shading and temperature effects.

The engineering problem is also spatial. Singapore has limited land and competing uses for roofs and open space. A conventional flat PV array uses horizontal footprint efficiently in a simple and mature way, but a three-dimensional canopy can place more active PV surface above the same footprint. That does not automatically mean more useful electricity: extra surface can face away from the Sun, shade other surface, see less sky, run hotter, require more structure, or consume energy if it tracks. The founding mushroom-head idea is therefore treated as a candidate to test rather than an assumed improvement.

A useful mental model for the rest of this report is
\begin{equation}
\text{useful PV energy}\;\approx\;\text{available sunlight}\times\text{captured fraction}\times\text{conversion efficiency}\;-\;\text{system losses}.
\label{eq:beginner-energy-chain}
\end{equation}
The following sections turn each phrase into explicit geometry and physics. Solar position determines where the direct beam comes from; facet normals determine whether a surface faces it; visibility and self-shading determine whether the light reaches the surface; diffuse-sky models account for the rest of the sky; temperature and electrical models convert received irradiance to electricity; and mechanical models account for the cost and feasibility of moving structures.

\subsection{From irradiance to the geometry equations}
The beginner picture above can now be converted into the quantities used by the mathematical model. At a given instant the atmosphere supplies an irradiance field. For an unobstructed horizontal receiver, global horizontal irradiance (GHI), direct normal irradiance (DNI), diffuse horizontal irradiance (DHI), and solar elevation \(\alpha\) satisfy the closure relation
\begin{equation}
GHI = DNI\,\sin\alpha + DHI.
\label{eq:irradiance-closure}
\end{equation}
Here \(GHI\) is global horizontal irradiance (\si{\watt\per\metre\squared}); \(DNI\) is direct normal irradiance (\si{\watt\per\metre\squared}); \(DHI\) is diffuse horizontal irradiance (\si{\watt\per\metre\squared}); and \(\alpha\) is solar elevation above the local horizon (rad in the numerical implementation). The sine factor is dimensionless, so every term has units of \si{\watt\per\metre\squared}. This relation is a measurement/model consistency check; it is not a complete irradiance-transposition model for an arbitrarily oriented PV surface.

A three-dimensional geometry changes what happens after the atmospheric irradiance field is specified. Each small PV surface element has an outward unit normal \(\mathbf n\), while the direction from that element toward the Sun is the unit vector \(\mathbf s\). Their dot product gives the cosine projection of the direct beam,
\begin{equation}
\cos\theta_i=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s,
\label{eq:incidence-bridge}
\end{equation}
where \(\theta_i\) is the incidence angle between the receiving-surface normal and the Sun direction (rad or degrees under a consistent convention), and \(\mathbf n\) and \(\mathbf s\) are dimensionless unit vectors. For a monofacial front surface, a negative dot product means that the direct beam lies behind the active face, motivating the positive-part operator used later.

Orientation is not sufficient by itself. Another part of the canopy may intercept the ray, so the direct model also requires visibility \(V\). Diffuse radiation requires a sky-view model because a tilted or crowded facet may see only part of the sky. Received irradiance is subsequently converted to electrical power using a PV conversion model, with later corrections for temperature, bifacial response, electrical mismatch and system losses.

The modelling chain is therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{weather} &\rightarrow \text{Sun position}
\rightarrow \text{surface orientation}
\rightarrow \text{visibility/sky view}\\
&\rightarrow \text{received irradiance}
\rightarrow \text{electrical energy}
\rightarrow \text{land-normalised comparison}
\end{aligned}}
\label{eq:model-chain}
\end{equation}
This chain is the bridge between the physical explanation and the equations below. It also explains why curved surface area alone cannot establish a performance advantage: additional PV area increases packing only if that area retains sufficient irradiance productivity after orientation, occlusion and system losses are included.

\section{Coordinate and sign conventions}
The canonical local frame is right-handed East--North--Up (ENU): \(+x\) east, \(+y\) north and \(+z\) vertically upward. A position vector is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r=(x,y,z).
\label{eq:position-enu}
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathbf r\) is position (\si{\metre}); \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) are the east, north and upward coordinates respectively (\si{\metre}). These axes are project definitions rather than empirical parameters.

Solar and facet azimuth are measured clockwise from geographic north: north \(=0^\circ\), east \(=90^\circ\), south \(=180^\circ\), west \(=270^\circ\). The canonical Rust implementation uses radians internally. For solar elevation \(\alpha\) and solar azimuth \(\gamma_s\), the ENU unit vector toward the Sun is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf s=
\left(
\cos\alpha\sin\gamma_s,\,
\cos\alpha\cos\gamma_s,\,
\sin\alpha
\right).
\label{eq:sun-vector-enu}
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathbf s\) is the dimensionless unit vector from the receiving point toward the Sun; \(\alpha\) is solar elevation above the local horizon (rad in Rust); and \(\gamma_s\) is solar azimuth clockwise from north (rad in Rust). At zenith Eq.~\eqref{eq:sun-vector-enu} gives \((0,0,1)\); at the eastern horizon it gives \((1,0,0)\); at the northern horizon it gives \((0,1,0)\). Its norm is unity.

For facet tilt \(\beta\) from horizontal and facet azimuth \(\gamma_p\),
\begin{equation}
\mathbf n=
\left(
\sin\beta\sin\gamma_p,\,
\sin\beta\cos\gamma_p,\,
\cos\beta
\right).
\label{eq:facet-normal-enu}
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathbf n\) is the outward/front-side unit normal (dimensionless); \(\beta\) is facet tilt from horizontal (rad in Rust); and \(\gamma_p\) is facet azimuth clockwise from north (rad in Rust).

The ENU convention makes the vertical component directly represent upward projection. An upward horizontal module has \(\mathbf n=(0,0,1)\).

Geometry generators, Sun vectors, facet normals, visibility rays and future mechanical axes must use this convention. External datasets or libraries using different azimuth definitions must be converted at the data boundary.

\section{Solar-position equations}
For preliminary transparent calculations, the Cooper (1969) declination approximation is
\begin{equation}
\delta(n)=23.45^{\circ}\sin\!\left[\frac{360^{\circ}}{365}(284+n)\right].
\label{eq:cooper}
\end{equation}
Here $\delta$ is solar declination (degrees); $n$ is ordinal day of a non-leap year (dimensionless day index); $23.45^{\circ}$ is the approximate declination amplitude in the Cooper engineering correlation; $360^{\circ}$ is one complete angular cycle; $365$ is the non-leap-year period assumed by the approximation; and $284$ is its phase-offset constant. Equation~\eqref{eq:cooper} is an astronomical approximation, not an exact identity or a project-fitted model. Validated simulations should use a traceable higher-accuracy solar-position method.

Using local apparent solar time,
\begin{equation}
H=15^{\circ}\,\si{\per\hour}\left(t_{\mathrm{solar}}-12\,\si{\hour}\right).
\label{eq:hour-angle}
\end{equation}
Here $H$ is solar hour angle (degrees); $t_{\mathrm{solar}}$ is local apparent solar time (hours); $15^{\circ}\,\si{\per\hour}$ follows from $360^{\circ}/24\,\si{\hour}$; and $12\,\si{\hour}$ denotes apparent solar noon, where $H=0^{\circ}$. Civil clock time must not be substituted directly without longitude and equation-of-time correction.

Solar elevation satisfies
\begin{equation}
\sin\alpha=\sin\phi\sin\delta+\cos\phi\cos\delta\cos H.
\label{eq:elevation}
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha$ is solar elevation above the local horizon; $\phi$ is geographic latitude; $\delta$ is solar declination; and $H$ is solar hour angle. All angular quantities must use a consistent convention; the numerical implementation converts angles to radians before evaluating trigonometric functions.

\section{Direct and diffuse incidence}
For an infinitesimal PV element,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm dP_{\mathrm{dir}}=\eta(T)DNI(t)V(t)[\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s(t)]_{+}\,\mathrm dA.
\label{eq:direct}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathrm dP_{\mathrm{dir}}$ is incremental direct-derived electrical power (W); $\eta(T)$ is conversion efficiency (dimensionless); $DNI$ is direct normal irradiance (\si{\watt\per\square\metre}); $V$ is direct visibility (dimensionless); $\mathbf n$ and $\mathbf s$ are unit vectors (dimensionless); $[x]_+=\max(0,x)$ is the positive-part operator; and $\mathrm dA$ is active PV area (\si{\square\metre}). The unit check is $(\si{\watt\per\square\metre})(\si{\square\metre})=\si{\watt}$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includesvg[width=0.90\textwidth]{../figures/direct_incidence_geometry}
\caption{Direct-beam incidence convention for a PV facet. The front-side contribution is proportional to $[\mathbf n\!\cdot\!\mathbf s]_+$ and visibility $V$; grazing and backside incidence contribute zero to the present monofacial direct term.}
\label{fig:direct-incidence}
\end{figure}

The first diffuse approximation is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm dP_{\mathrm{diff}}=\eta(T)DHI(t)\frac{1+\cos\beta}{2}\,\mathrm dA.
\label{eq:diffuse}
\end{equation}
Here $DHI$ is diffuse horizontal irradiance (\si{\watt\per\square\metre}); $\beta$ is surface tilt from horizontal; and $(1+\cos\beta)/2$ is the unobstructed isotropic-sky view factor. The factor $1/2$ follows from ideal hemispherical geometry and is not an empirical Singapore coefficient.

\section{Paraboloidal mushroom}
The founding analytical geometry is
\begin{equation}
z(r)=h\left(1-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right),\qquad k=\frac{h}{R}.
\label{eq:surface}
\end{equation}
Here $z$ is surface height (m); $r$ is radial coordinate (m); $h$ is apex height (m); $R$ is footprint radius (m); and $k$ is dimensionless curvature/aspect ratio.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includesvg[width=0.90\textwidth]{../figures/paraboloid_geometry}
\caption{Labelled cross-section of the founding analytical paraboloidal mushroom cap. The diagram identifies footprint radius $R$, apex height $h$, radial coordinate $r$, surface height $z(r)$ and a representative local outward normal. Geometry only---not a validated optimum or performance result.}
\label{fig:mushroom-geometry}
\end{figure}

Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:surface},
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm dz}{\mathrm dr}=-\frac{2hr}{R^2}.
\end{equation}
The slope is dimensionless. The numerical factor 2 arises exactly from differentiating $r^2$.

The axisymmetric area is
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{PV}}=2\pi\int_0^Rr\sqrt{1+\frac{4h^2r^2}{R^4}}\,\mathrm dr.
\end{equation}
Here $A_{\mathrm{PV}}$ is active curved area (\si{\square\metre}); $2\pi r\,\mathrm dr$ is the annular area contribution; and the square-root term is the dimensionless slope correction.

Exact integration gives
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{PV}}=\frac{\pi R^2}{6k^2}\left[(1+4k^2)^{3/2}-1\right].
\end{equation}
The factors 4 and 6 arise from the derivative and exact integral, not empirical fitting.

With $A_{\mathrm{foot}}=\pi R^2$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Pi=\frac{A_{\mathrm{PV}}}{A_{\mathrm{foot}}}=\frac{(1+4k^2)^{3/2}-1}{6k^2}}.
\label{eq:packing-paraboloid}
\end{equation}
Here $\Pi$ is the dimensionless PV packing ratio and $A_{\mathrm{foot}}$ is horizontal footprint area (\si{\square\metre}). The limiting case $k\to0$ gives $\Pi\to1$, recovering a flat disk.

\section{Diffuse-light analytical limit}
Under the unobstructed isotropic-sky benchmark, the diffuse-derived electrical power over surface \(S\) is
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{diff}}
=
\eta DHI
\int_S
\frac{1+\cos\beta}{2}\,\mathrm dA.
\label{eq:diffuse-integrated}
\end{equation}
Here \(P_{\mathrm{diff}}\) is diffuse-derived electrical power (W); \(\eta\) is photovoltaic conversion efficiency (dimensionless); \(DHI\) is diffuse horizontal irradiance (\si{\watt\per\square\metre}); \(\beta\) is local surface tilt from horizontal; \(S\) is the receiving PV surface; and \(\mathrm dA\) is differential active area (\si{\square\metre}). The factor \(1/2\) is the ideal hemispherical isotropic-sky coefficient, not a Singapore empirical parameter.

Separating the integral gives
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{diff}}
=
\frac{\eta DHI}{2}
\left(
\int_S\mathrm dA
+
\int_S\cos\beta\,\mathrm dA
\right).
\end{equation}
For a single-valued upward-facing graph surface with no overhangs, its vertical projection satisfies
\begin{equation}
\int_S\cos\beta\,\mathrm dA=A_{\mathrm{foot}}.
\label{eq:vertical-projection}
\end{equation}
Here \(A_{\mathrm{foot}}\) is the horizontal projected footprint (\si{\square\metre}). Since \(\int_S\mathrm dA=A_{\mathrm{PV}}\), the benchmark becomes
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
P_{\mathrm{diff}}
=
\frac{\eta DHI}{2}
\left(
A_{\mathrm{PV}}+A_{\mathrm{foot}}
\right)
}.
\label{eq:diffuse-cap-limit}
\end{equation}
The unit check is
\((\si{\watt\per\square\metre})(\si{\square\metre})=\si{\watt}\).

\paragraph{Physical Interpretation.}
For this idealised convex/no-occlusion benchmark, additional upward-facing surface area can intercept additional isotropic diffuse sky radiation while the projected-area term remains fixed by the footprint. This does not imply that arbitrarily increasing curvature produces a real annual-yield gain because real facets obscure portions of the sky and may obstruct one another.

\paragraph{Engineering Implication.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:diffuse-cap-limit} is an analytical benchmark for later Rust and ray/sky-view implementations. It must not be used as a validated Singapore annual-yield model. Final 3-D calculations require time-correlated DHI, an anisotropic sky treatment and explicit sky visibility.

\section{Land multiplication}
For an equal-land comparison, define the land multiplication factor
\begin{equation}
M_L=\frac{E_{3D}}{E_{\mathrm{base}}}.
\label{eq:land-multiplication-definition}
\end{equation}
Here \(M_L\) is land multiplication (dimensionless); \(E_{3D}\) is annual electrical energy from the candidate over the common land footprint (\si{\kilo\watt\hour\per\year}); and \(E_{\mathrm{base}}\) is annual electrical energy from the explicitly defined baseline over the same footprint (\si{\kilo\watt\hour\per\year}).

Define the packing ratio
\begin{equation}
\Pi=\frac{A_{\mathrm{PV}}}{A_{\mathrm{land}}}.
\label{eq:packing-land}
\end{equation}
Here \(\Pi\) is dimensionless; \(A_{\mathrm{PV}}\) is active photovoltaic area (\si{\square\metre}); and \(A_{\mathrm{land}}\) is constrained horizontal site area (\si{\square\metre}).

Multiplying and dividing Eq.~\eqref{eq:land-multiplication-definition} by \(\Pi\) gives
\begin{equation}
M_L=\Pi\frac{E_{3D}}{\Pi E_{\mathrm{base}}}.
\end{equation}
All terms are dimensionless because the energy ratio and \(\Pi\) are dimensionless. Define packed-PV productivity
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}=\frac{E_{3D}}{\Pi E_{\mathrm{base}}}.
\label{eq:packing-productivity}
\end{equation}
Here \(\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}\) is average packed-PV productivity relative to the baseline (dimensionless). Therefore
\begin{equation}
\boxed{M_L=\Pi\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}}.
\label{eq:land-product}
\end{equation}
Here \(M_L\), \(\Pi\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}\) are dimensionless.

For a differentiable relationship \(\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}(\Pi)\),
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm dM_L}{\mathrm d\Pi}
=
\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}
+
\Pi\frac{\mathrm d\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}}{\mathrm d\Pi}.
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathrm dM_L/\mathrm d\Pi\) and \(\mathrm d\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}/\mathrm d\Pi\) are dimensionless derivatives. An interior stationary point of the land-energy objective satisfies
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}
+
\Pi\frac{\mathrm d\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}}{\mathrm d\Pi}
=0,
\end{equation}
which can be written
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
-\frac{\mathrm d\ln\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}}{\mathrm d\ln\Pi}=1
}.
\end{equation}
The logarithmic derivative is dimensionless. The numerical value 1 follows exactly from differentiating the product in Eq.~\eqref{eq:land-product}; it is not an empirical threshold.

\paragraph{Physical Interpretation.}
\(\Pi\) measures how much active PV is packed into scarce footprint, whereas \(\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}\) measures how productive that packed PV remains. Increasing surface area alone can therefore raise \(\Pi\) while lowering \(\eta_{\mathrm{pack}}\).

\paragraph{Engineering Implication.}
A three-dimensional design is attractive for land use only when packing gain outweighs orientation, occlusion, thermal, structural, maintenance and economic penalties. The stationary condition is a diagnostic for later packing sweeps, not a validated project optimum.

\section{Sphere baseline}
A complete sphere provides a directionally symmetric analytical comparison. Its total surface area is
\begin{equation}
A_s=4\pi R^2,
\label{eq:sphere-area}
\end{equation}
where \(A_s\) is spherical surface area (\si{\square\metre}) and \(R\) is sphere radius (\si{\metre}). The factor 4 and \(\pi\) are exact geometric constants.

For parallel rays arriving from any direction, the orthogonal projected area of the sphere is the area of its circular silhouette,
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{proj}}=\pi R^2.
\label{eq:sphere-projected-area}
\end{equation}
Here \(A_{\mathrm{proj}}\) is projected area normal to the beam (\si{\square\metre}). Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\frac{A_s}{A_{\mathrm{proj}}}=4.
\end{equation}
This ratio is dimensionless and follows from exact sphere geometry; it is not an energy-yield multiplier.

\paragraph{Physical Interpretation.}
A sphere presents the same projected silhouette to a collimated beam regardless of beam direction while distributing its total surface over all outward orientations. Much of that surface is therefore oblique to, or on the back side of, any instantaneous direct beam.

\paragraph{Engineering Implication.}
The sphere is useful as a symmetry and packing benchmark, not as evidence of four-times energy collection. Any fair PV comparison must separately account for active-area budget, front/back electrical behaviour, self/structural shading, diffuse sky access and land footprint.

\section{Mechanics}
Angular momentum and rotational kinetic energy are
\begin{equation}
L=I\omega,\qquad E_k=\frac12I\omega^2.
\end{equation}
Here $L$ is angular momentum (\si{\kilogram\metre\squared\per\second}); $I$ is mass moment of inertia (\si{\kilogram\metre\squared}); $\omega$ is angular velocity (\si{\radian\per\second}); and $E_k$ is rotational kinetic energy (J). The factor $1/2$ is the exact rigid-body kinetic-energy coefficient.

A first torque balance is
\begin{equation}
\tau=I\ddot\theta+\tau_f+\tau_g+\tau_w.
\end{equation}
Here $\tau$ is actuator torque (N m); $\ddot\theta$ is angular acceleration (rad s$^{-2}$); and $\tau_f$, $\tau_g$, $\tau_w$ are frictional, gravitational and wind torques (N m).

The first drag approximation is
\begin{equation}
F_D=\frac12\rho C_DA_{\mathrm{proj}}v^2.
\end{equation}
Here $F_D$ is drag force (N); $\rho$ is air density (kg m$^{-3}$); $C_D$ is dimensionless drag coefficient; $A_{\mathrm{proj}}$ is projected area (m$^2$); and $v$ is relative wind speed (m s$^{-1}$). The $1/2$ is the standard dynamic-pressure coefficient.

A characteristic inertial-torque scaling for a movement through angular displacement \(\Delta\theta\) over duration \(T\) is
\begin{equation}
\tau_I\sim I\frac{\Delta\theta}{T^2}.
\end{equation}
Here \(\tau_I\) is characteristic inertial torque (\si{\newton\metre}); \(I\) is mass moment of inertia (\si{\kilogram\metre\squared}); \(\Delta\theta\) is angular displacement (rad, dimensionless in SI); and \(T\) is movement duration (\si{\second}). For the same displacement executed in twice the time,
\begin{equation}
\tau_I(2T)\sim\frac{1}{4}\tau_I(T).
\end{equation}
Here the factor \(1/4\) follows exactly from the \(T^{-2}\) scaling.

\paragraph{Physical Interpretation.}
The Sun moves slowly on actuator timescales, so deliberate tracking can be quasi-static. Increasing movement duration strongly suppresses inertial torque.

\paragraph{Engineering Implication.}
The original momentum question should not become an objective to maximise angular momentum. Later actuator design should prioritise wind moment, gravitational balance, friction, locking/stow, efficiency, reliability and wear. Quantitative mechanical conclusions remain blocked until the required physical parameters in the provenance register are sourced or calculated.

\section{Tracking as an optimal-control extension}
Tracking is treated as a net-value control problem rather than as an instruction to maximise angular momentum. A generic objective is
\begin{equation}
\max_{\theta(t)}
\left\{
\int_T
\left[
P_{\mathrm{PV}}(\theta,t)
-
P_{\mathrm{motor}}(\theta,\dot\theta,\ddot\theta)
\right]\,\mathrm dt
-
C_{\mathrm{wear}}
\right\}.
\label{eq:tracking-control}
\end{equation}
Here \(\theta(t)\) is tracker orientation (rad); \(P_{\mathrm{PV}}\) is photovoltaic electrical power (W); \(P_{\mathrm{motor}}\) is actuator electrical power (W); \(T\) is the control/optimisation time horizon; and \(C_{\mathrm{wear}}\) is a wear penalty expressed in energy-equivalent units, or converted consistently if the final objective is monetary.

The time integral of power has units of energy. Therefore \(C_{\mathrm{wear}}\) must use compatible units before subtraction; a monetary wear term cannot be subtracted directly from joules or watt-hours without an explicit conversion.

\paragraph{Physical Interpretation.}
Because solar motion is slow, useful tracking may consist of infrequent or quasi-static repositioning. Additional motion is not automatically beneficial: actuator consumption, wind exposure, wear, stow events and reliability can outweigh small irradiance gains.

\paragraph{Engineering Implication.}
The eventual controller must compare fixed, scheduled-step and continuous tracking under the same geometry/resource envelope. Equation~\eqref{eq:tracking-control} is a formulation only; no optimal tracking schedule is presently claimed.

\section{Bifacial and thermal extensions}
For a bifacial facet \(i\), define effective irradiance
\begin{equation}
G_{\mathrm{eff},i}
=
G_{\mathrm{front},i}
+
bG_{\mathrm{rear},i}.
\label{eq:bifacial-effective}
\end{equation}
Here \(G_{\mathrm{eff},i}\), \(G_{\mathrm{front},i}\) and \(G_{\mathrm{rear},i}\) are irradiances (\si{\watt\per\square\metre}), and \(b\) is the dimensionless bifaciality factor. The equation is dimensionally consistent because both additive terms have irradiance units.

A first linear temperature correction is
\begin{equation}
\eta(T_c)
=
\eta_{\mathrm{ref}}
\left[
1+\gamma(T_c-T_{\mathrm{ref}})
\right].
\label{eq:temperature-efficiency}
\end{equation}
Here \(T_c\) is cell temperature; \(T_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is reference cell temperature on the same temperature scale; \(\eta_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is reference conversion efficiency (dimensionless); and \(\gamma\) is the relative temperature coefficient (\si{\per\kelvin}). The product \(\gamma(T_c-T_{\mathrm{ref}})\) is dimensionless.

\paragraph{Physical Interpretation.}
A three-dimensional canopy changes both rear-surface exposure and thermal environment. Additional packed area may improve rear-sky/ground access in some geometries while dense packing may also reduce ventilation or create mutual shading.

\paragraph{Engineering Implication.}
Neither Eq.~\eqref{eq:bifacial-effective} nor Eq.~\eqref{eq:temperature-efficiency} is a complete bifacial or thermal model. Validated use requires a selected module, sourced bifaciality and temperature coefficients, front/rear view and reflection modelling, ambient weather, and an appropriate cell-temperature model. Those inputs remain unset in the provenance register.

A_{\mathrm{foot}}\le\SI{1}{\square\metre},\qquad
\sum_iA_i\le\SI{2}{\square\metre},\qquad
0\le z_i\le\SI{2}{\metre}.
\end{equation}
Here $A_{\mathrm{foot}}$ is footprint area; $A_i$ is facet area; and $z_i$ is facet elevation. The numerical values 1, 2 and 2 are engineering design assumptions selected to define a reproducible comparison. They are neither Singapore regulatory limits nor discovered optima and must be varied in sensitivity analysis.


\section{Parameter evidence status and model-input gate}

A_{\mathrm{foot}}\le\SI{1}{\square\metre},\qquad
A_{\mathrm{PV}}\le\SI{2}{\square\metre},\qquad
H_{\max}\le\SI{2}{\metre}.
\label{eq:resource-envelope}
\end{equation}
Here \(A_{\mathrm{foot}}\) is the horizontal footprint budget (\si{\square\metre}); \(A_{\mathrm{PV}}\) is the active photovoltaic-area budget (\si{\square\metre}); and \(H_{\max}\) is the maximum candidate height (\si{\metre}). The numerical values 1, 2 and 2 are engineering comparison assumptions inherited from the foundation experiment. They are neither Singapore regulatory limits nor discovered optima and require sensitivity sweeps before design conclusions.

For an upper hemisphere,
\begin{equation}
A_h=2\pi R^2,\qquad A_{\mathrm{foot}}=\pi R^2,\qquad
\frac{A_h}{A_{\mathrm{foot}}}=2.
\end{equation}
This is an exact geometric area ratio, not an energy multiplier.

The non-smooth candidates use one common triangular-facet representation. For facet \(i\),
\begin{equation}
\mathbf e_{i1}=\mathbf v_{i2}-\mathbf v_{i1},\qquad
\mathbf e_{i2}=\mathbf v_{i3}-\mathbf v_{i1},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
A_i=\frac12\left\|\mathbf e_{i1}\times\mathbf e_{i2}\right\|,\qquad
\mathbf n_i=
\frac{\mathbf e_{i1}\times\mathbf e_{i2}}
{\left\|\mathbf e_{i1}\times\mathbf e_{i2}\right\|}.
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathbf v_{ij}\) are ENU vertex positions (m), \(A_i\) is facet area (\si{\square\metre}), and \(\mathbf n_i\) is the outward dimensionless unit normal. Total active area is
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{PV}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_i.
\end{equation}
The direct contribution is assembled consistently for every candidate as
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{dir}}(t)=
\eta(T)DNI(t)
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
A_iV_i(t)[\mathbf n_i\cdot\mathbf s(t)]_+.
\end{equation}

A radial flower/petal placement may initially use
\begin{equation}
\gamma_j=\gamma_0+\frac{2\pi j}{N_p},\qquad j=0,\ldots,N_p-1,
\end{equation}
where \(N_p\) is petal count and \(\gamma_j\) is petal azimuth. Equal angular spacing is a reproducible starting parameterisation, not a discovered optimum.

An east--west folded first experiment uses opposing facet azimuths
\begin{equation}
\gamma_{p,+}=90^\circ,\qquad \gamma_{p,-}=270^\circ,
\end{equation}
with common or independently varied tilt magnitudes. Fold pitch and ridge height must be included in shading and footprint calculations.

For free-form facets, retain
\begin{equation}
\mathbf X=[\mathbf x_1,\ldots,\mathbf x_N],\qquad
\mathbf x_i=(x_i,y_i,z_i,\theta_i,\phi_i,A_i),
\end{equation}
and eventually maximise \(E_{\mathrm{net,annual}}(\mathbf X)\) subject to the common resource envelope, non-overlap, structural, access, electrical and manufacturability constraints. Free-form optimisation remains deliberately downstream of solar-position, irradiance, visibility, uncertainty and convergence validation.

\paragraph{Physical Interpretation.}
These candidates test different mechanisms: directional symmetry, discrete manufacturable normals, sparse sky/rear access, orientation diversity, and unconstrained geometric search. None is expected to win from geometry alone.

\paragraph{Engineering Implication.}
The same weather, optical kernel, active-area accounting, footprint definition and electrical/loss assumptions must evaluate all candidates. Otherwise a shape comparison confounds geometry with unequal resources or model assumptions.

\section{Limitations and engineering interpretation}
The analytical work already answers structural questions without claiming a winning geometry. Curvature can increase active surface area within a fixed footprint, but area alone is not energy. The land-multiplication identity separates packing from average PV productivity. Sphere and hemisphere results are symmetry and packing benchmarks rather than multiplicative-yield claims. Slow solar tracking reduces the importance of inertial torque relative to wind, balance, friction, locking, wear and actuator efficiency. A common facet representation allows named and free-form candidates to be evaluated by the same optical kernel.

The unresolved questions are quantitative: how much useful irradiance each packed surface retains under Singapore weather; how much is lost to mutual shading and reduced sky view; whether bifacial access and ventilation compensate for those losses; and whether any electrical gain survives structural, mechanical and lifecycle penalties.

\section{Two-technique solution strategy}
The mathematical core is developed through two independent routes so that the numerical geometry can be verified before it is used on shapes without closed-form solutions.

\subsection{Technique 1: analytical route}
For geometries admitting closed-form treatment, the surface remains continuous. The paraboloidal mushroom provides the principal verification target:
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{PV}}
=\frac{\pi R^2}{6k^2}
\left[(1+4k^2)^{3/2}-1\right],
\qquad k=\frac{h}{R}.
\end{equation}
Under the ideal isotropic unobstructed diffuse benchmark,
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{diff}}
=\frac{\eta DHI}{2}
\left(A_{\mathrm{PV}}+A_{\mathrm{foot}}\right).
\end{equation}
These exact expressions are verification targets rather than annual Singapore-yield predictions.

\subsection{Technique 2: discrete surface route}
The same surface is independently approximated by triangular facets. For triangle $i$,
\begin{equation}
A_i=\frac12\|\mathbf e_{i1}\times\mathbf e_{i2}\|,
\qquad
\mathbf n_i=
\frac{\mathbf e_{i1}\times\mathbf e_{i2}}
{\|\mathbf e_{i1}\times\mathbf e_{i2}\|}.
\end{equation}
The discrete area and ideal diffuse benchmark are
\begin{equation}
A_{\mathrm{PV}}^{(N)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_i,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{diff}}^{(N)}
=
\frac{\eta DHI}{2}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_i(1+n_{z,i}).
\end{equation}
Verification requires
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}A_{\mathrm{PV}}^{(N)}
=A_{\mathrm{PV}}^{(\mathrm{analytical})}
}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}P_{\mathrm{diff}}^{(N)}
=P_{\mathrm{diff}}^{(\mathrm{analytical})}.
}
\end{equation}
The canonical Rust binary \texttt{discrete-verification} performs this refinement experiment. After verification, the same facet machinery can be applied to folded, petal, faceted and free-form candidates.

\subsection{Role of FTCS}
FTCS is not used as the primary geometry method because the present problem is a surface-integration/visibility problem rather than a diffusion-type time-dependent PDE. FTCS becomes relevant if a spatially resolved transient module-temperature model is introduced, for example
\begin{equation}
\rho c_p\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
=
k_T\nabla^2T+\dot q.
\end{equation}
Such a thermal model would require its own explicit-scheme stability criterion and spatial/time convergence study; no FTCS thermal result is currently claimed.

\section{Numerical method and reproducibility roadmap}
The numerical implementation is deliberately layered so that each physical addition can be tested before it affects an annual comparison. Geometry is represented in the canonical ENU frame. Analytical shapes provide exact benchmarks; general candidates are discretised into planar facets. For each timestamp the solar-position layer supplies the Sun direction, the irradiance layer supplies compatible direct and diffuse inputs, and the geometry layer supplies facet positions, areas and normals. Visibility and sky-view calculations then determine which radiation reaches each facet before thermal/electrical conversion and time integration.

A future mesh calculation must demonstrate convergence. If a candidate is represented with characteristic mesh resolution $h_m$, an output quantity $Q$ must be shown to approach a stable value as $h_m$ decreases. The project will therefore report a sequence such as $Q(h_m)$ rather than selecting one visually smooth mesh and assuming it is accurate. The same principle applies to sky-patch resolution, time step and optimisation tolerances.

The canonical implementation language is Rust. Reproducibility requires the exact repository commit, input-data manifest, parameter/provenance register, command line, test result and generated-output identity to be retained. A successful executable run is necessary but not sufficient for physical validation.

\section{Singapore data and validation requirements}
The annual model requires time-correlated Singapore irradiance rather than a single annual irradiation number. At minimum the weather contract must provide or support traceable derivation of GHI, DHI and DNI on a declared timestamp convention, together with sufficient site metadata for solar position. Ambient temperature, wind and other fields become required when the corresponding thermal or structural model is activated.

Input validation begins with timestamp/schema checks and irradiance consistency. For compatible observations, the horizontal closure relation
\begin{equation}
GHI \approx DNI\sin\alpha + DHI
\end{equation}
provides a diagnostic residual, with any numerical acceptance tolerance required to be justified rather than invented. Solar position must be benchmarked against an authoritative reference before the residual can be interpreted as a weather-data quality metric.

The project must then validate model layers in increasing complexity: cardinal-vector and limiting-case tests; analytical paraboloid and hemisphere geometry; direct-incidence front/grazing/back cases; irradiance closure; plane-of-array transposition; visibility/ray tests; diffuse sky-view benchmarks; thermal/electrical checks; and finally annual integration. Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis follow only after the corresponding input/model layer exists.

\section{Planned comparative results format}
When the validation gates close, every candidate will be reported under the same resource envelope and weather record. The comparison table will contain, at minimum: active PV area, land footprint, packing ratio, annual gross PV energy, annual actuator/auxiliary energy where applicable, annual net energy, land-normalised net energy, PV-area-normalised productivity, and uncertainty or sensitivity bounds. Structural/cost quantities will be added only when their models and inputs are sufficiently defined.

The report will also include convergence plots for mesh/time/sky resolution, representative shading/sky-view renders, annual or monthly energy distributions, and a packing-productivity trade-off plot. These are specified deliverables, not currently available evidence, and therefore are not populated with synthetic numbers in this current-state report.

\section{Current technical conclusions}
At the present evidence level, the project supports conclusions about formulation rather than a winning design. Three-dimensional PV geometry is fundamentally a packing-versus-productivity problem under constrained land. The paraboloid provides an exact curved-surface benchmark, while sphere and hemisphere geometries provide symmetry and area/projection benchmarks. Faceted, petal, folded and free-form designs can be evaluated through one common mesh representation. Slow tracking should be assessed through net energy, wind loading, balance, wear and reliability rather than by maximising angular momentum.

No candidate is currently established as optimal for Singapore. That conclusion is intentional: the next meaningful advance is a validated annual irradiance/visibility/electrical chain followed by equal-resource comparison, not an unsupported optimisation result.
